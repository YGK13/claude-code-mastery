**The stake.** A CAIO I mentored took the seat at a $5B logistics company last spring. Brilliant operator, perfect resume, came in with energy. ## The principle: listen before you ship, then ship something small

The trap every new CAIO falls into is the same. You were hired because the company believes AI is urgent. You feel pressure to demonstrate motion. You launch. Six months later, the things you launched do not stitch into a strategy and the things you did not launch are the things that mattered.

The discipline of the first 100 days is the opposite of what the role pressure tells you. You will listen for 30 days, design for 30 and ship one carefully chosen thing in the last 40. That is the cadence that builds the foundation for the next three years.

The board does not need to see five wins in the first quarter. The board needs to see that you have a strategy, a portfolio, a governance structure and one shipped proof point. Everything else is theatre.

## Days 1-30: the listen tour, gap audit and governance committee

The first 30 days are a listening engagement disguised as an executive role. You will resist this. Resist the resistance.

Week one. Meet your CEO and get clarity on three questions: what does success look like at the next board meeting, what is the one thing that would cause you to fail, and who in the organization should I be most worried about. Take the answers seriously. The third question is the most important. CAIO failures are almost always political, not technical.

Weeks two and three. The listen tour. 30 one-hour meetings with the people who will determine whether you succeed. The list, in priority order: your direct reports if you inherited any, the CFO, the CIO, the general counsel, the chief risk officer, the heads of your top three business units, the head of HR, the chief data officer if there is one, the head of internal audit, two to four respected ICs in the data and engineering orgs, and three to five external advisors or board members. The agenda is the same in every meeting: where are we using AI today, what is broken, what would unlock the most value, what should I not touch.

Week four. The gap audit. You now have enough to write a one-page state-of-AI memo. What models are in production. What teams are doing what. What the regulatory exposure looks like. Where the literacy gaps are. Where the data infrastructure breaks. This memo is for you, not the board, but you will use 80% of it in the first board update.

By end of week four, you have also stood up your governance committee. Five to seven members: you as chair, the general counsel, the CFO or their designee, the CISO, the head of HR, the chief risk officer and one business-unit GM rotating quarterly. Charter, decision rights and meeting cadence published. This is the single most important structural move of your first 30 days because it converts every future governance question from a debate into a process.

Two quick wins identified by end of day 30. Not launched. Identified. The criteria: shippable in 60-90 days, owned by a business unit head who wants the win, measurable in dollars and unlikely to fail for technical reasons. The two-win frame is deliberate. One win is fragile; two builds a pattern.

## Days 31-60: operating model, portfolio of 12, first proof

Days 31-60 are when you stop listening and start designing. You will publish three artifacts.

The AI operating model. One page. Where AI lives in the company (your team, the business units, central platforms), how initiatives get funded, how they get killed, who decides what gets shipped, how risk gets reviewed, how literacy gets delivered. This is the document that turns chaos into a system. Show it to your CEO before you publish. Walk it through every direct report before you publish. By the time it goes wide, nobody should be surprised.

The portfolio of 12 bets. Sequenced across three horizons. Horizon one: four to five initiatives in production or near-production within 12 months, each tied to a P&L line. Horizon two: four to five initiatives in active build, 12-24 month payoff, requiring infrastructure or capability investment. Horizon three: two to three exploratory bets, 24-36 months, with clear kill criteria. The portfolio frame matters because <span class="pl-stat">95%</span> of enterprise AI pilots fail — MIT NANDA — and the board needs to see that you are running a portfolio with hit-rate logic, not a series of one-shot bets.

The first proof case shipped. By day 60, one of the two quick wins is live. Production. Real users. Real measurement. Not a demo. The win does not have to be transformational. It has to be undeniable. A customer-service deflection model that takes 8% out of contact volume. An invoice-coding model that takes three FTEs out of finance ops. A sales-enablement assistant that adds a measurable point of pipeline conversion. The point is that you shipped, and that the business owner ships with you in front of the company.

## Days 61-100: first board update, hiring plan, year-one budget

The final 40 days are about converting motion into mandate.

The board update. You get 15 minutes at the next board meeting. The deck is the five-slide structure from the previous lesson: the so-what (now with one quarter of internal data), the bet (the 12-initiative portfolio), the governance frame (your stood-up committee with one quarter of decisions), the ask (year-one budget defense) and the 100-day artifact (the proof case shipped). This is the meeting where the board decides whether you are an executive or an experiment. Pre-wire every director.

The hiring plan executed. The first three hires of a CAIO matter disproportionately because they become the institutional voice of the function. The order matters.

Hire one: a chief of staff or AI program manager. Senior, not junior. This person runs the operating cadence, owns the portfolio tracker, drafts the board materials and frees you to be in the field. Without this hire, you are doing your own administration in month four. Salary band $180K-$250K, often pulled from internal strategy or PMO.

Hire two: a head of AI engineering or AI platform lead. Technical, hands-on, capable of standing up the model inventory, the deployment infrastructure and the evaluation harness. This person makes the difference between portfolios that ship and portfolios that demo. Salary band $300K-$450K, often a senior engineering leader from a tech-forward company.

Hire three: a head of AI governance and risk. Comes from compliance, risk or audit. Owns the model risk framework, the regulatory tracking (EU AI Act Article 4 literacy live August 2026 is the first deadline most companies will face), the model inventory and the kill-switch protocols. Salary band $250K-$350K. The hire that makes the general counsel relax.

The first three hires define the first three years. Get them right and the next 10 hires are easy. Get them wrong and you will rebuild the function.

The year-one budget defended. By day 100, the budget you negotiated in the offer is now the budget you have to spend through the formal planning cycle. Defend it with three line items: people (the hiring plan above plus two to four ICs), platform (model access, evaluation infrastructure, observability tooling — typically $1M-$3M depending on scale), and portfolio (initiative-specific spend, usually structured as charge-backs to business units). The total should match what you negotiated. If finance pushes back, fight for the platform line. People you can defer. Portfolio you can re-allocate. Platform underspend in year one creates technical debt that takes three years to pay back.

## The wins to seek and the wins to avoid

Not all wins help you. The wrong wins kill your credibility faster than no wins.

Seek wins that have a named business-unit owner who shares the credit. The minute a CAIO claims sole credit for a P&L outcome, the business unit heads stop bringing you their problems. Always ship with a co-owner.

Seek wins that touch a real cost line or a real revenue line. A customer-service deflection that reduces contact volume by a measurable percentage. A pricing model that lifts margin in a defined segment. An automation that reduces a known FTE cost. The win should appear in the next quarterly financial review without needing a translator.

Seek wins that scale. A 30-person pilot in one region is a demo. A platform deployed to one business unit that the other three can adopt is a win. Optimize for the second.

Avoid vanity wins. The "first GenAI deployment" press release. The internal chatbot nobody uses after week three. The leaderboard-topping model that does not connect to a workflow. These wins look good in a board deck for one cycle and become liabilities in the next. The board remembers what you promised.

Avoid wins owned by your function alone. If only your team can run it, it is a science project. If your team gets hit by a bus and the system keeps running, it is a product.

Avoid wins that conflict with a powerful internal actor. The CFO's automation initiative, the CIO's data platform, the CMO's marketing-tech stack. These are battles you may eventually need to fight. You do not fight them in your first 100 days.

<details class="pl-check">
<summary>Check yourself</summary>
**Q.** Day 22 of your CAIO tenure. The CEO calls you in and says: "I need you to announce something big at the all-hands next Friday. Investors are asking about our AI story." What do you do?

**A.** You give the CEO something to announce that is real and small, not big and fake. The governance committee being stood up, the cross-functional portfolio review process, a partnership with a specific cost-line owner — these are credible announcements that do not commit you to outcomes you cannot deliver. You do not announce a product launch or a financial outcome at day 22. If the CEO insists on something larger, you push back in private: "What I announce now I have to defend at the next earnings call. Let me give you something bigger to announce at day 90 that we can actually deliver."
