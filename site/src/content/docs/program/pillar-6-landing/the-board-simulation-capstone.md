## What the capstone simulates

The capstone is a 60-minute simulated board moment. You walk in, present and defend a real CAIO mandate. The setup mirrors the highest-pressure version of a real first board meeting in the seat.

You are presenting five things, in order.

A strategy. The AI portfolio for the company you have chosen as your case — real public company, real industry context, real competitive set. You will have built the strategy across the program. The capstone is the defense, not the build.

A governance pack. The AI risk committee charter, the model inventory framework, the regulatory regime that applies (EU AI Act Article 4 literacy live as of August 2026 for any company touching the EU), the kill-switch protocols and the literacy training program. One page each, five pages total.

An ROI model. A defensible financial case for the year-one portfolio. Inputs documented. Sensitivity ranges shown. The number you are betting on is named and the assumption that would change it is named.

A portfolio shipped to date. Two to four artifacts you have built in the program — board memo, operating model, 100-day plan, segmentation of AI use cases, a deployed proof case. These are your evidence. Not described. Shown.

An ask. Budget, headcount, reporting line, decision rights. One slide. Specific numbers.

The room contains Yuri as chair, two cohort peers playing skeptical directors and one outside guest — a real sitting CAIO, a real public-company director or a real partner from a top executive search firm — playing the wildcard director. The wildcard is the one who asks the question that decides whether you pass.

## The rubric

You are scored on five dimensions. Each is rated 1-5. A pass requires a minimum of 3 on every dimension and a total of 20 or above.

Clarity. Can the room repeat your strategy back to you in one sentence. If not, you have not earned the mandate. This is the easiest dimension to underweight and the most common reason candidates fail. Operators who have lived inside a problem for months forget that the room is hearing it for the first time.

Command of the technical questions. Not "do you know the difference between fine-tuning and RAG." That is fluency, which the program assumes. Command means: when the wildcard director asks you to explain why you chose a specific architecture, you can defend it in language a non-technical CFO understands and a CTO respects. You hold both audiences in the same answer.

Governance answer. The moment a director asks about regulatory exposure, model risk or the literacy mandate, you have 30 seconds to demonstrate that you have already built the framework. Hesitation here is the single most disqualifying signal. <span class="pl-stat">76%</span> of CEOs in the IBM 2026 CEO Study now expect a CAIO seat. Almost none of them know what to ask. The director who does will be the one who decides whether you get hired.

Value case. The ROI model holds up under pressure. The assumptions are honest. The downside scenario is named. You are willing to give up the win if the numbers do not hold. The candidates who oversell here lose the room. The candidates who under-claim lose the seat.

Composure under pressure. The wildcard director will ask the question designed to break you. "Why should we trust your numbers when 95% of pilots fail." "Aren't you just rebranding the analytics team." "Walk us through what happens when your model causes a regulatory incident in production." Your job is not to answer perfectly. Your job is to remain the most thoughtful person in the room. The composure score is what separates a CAIO from a director of AI.

## What gets graded by Yuri and your peers

The capstone is graded in three passes.

Yuri scores the rubric in real time, using a structured form. The form becomes part of your portfolio. It is detailed enough that you can take it to a hiring committee and show them — many graduates have, with permission, included a redacted version in their executive search submissions. It functions as the only third-party performance signal on the market for CAIO candidates.

Your three peer panelists score independently using the same rubric. Peer scores are anonymized and aggregated. The peer signal matters because peers played the directors and saw your behavior from the other side of the table. Their scores frequently surface composure issues that Yuri, who is steering the conversation, does not see.

The wildcard director provides one written page of feedback. Not a score. A letter. What they would tell a hiring committee about you, written in the voice they would use if a search partner called them next week. This letter is the most valuable artifact of the entire program for many graduates.

## How to prepare in the four weeks leading up

The four-week prep arc is structured. Skip a phase and you will feel it in the room.

Week one. Rebuild the deck. The five slides from your board-memo lesson, refreshed with the artifacts you have built across the program. Time yourself. 20 minutes including questions means roughly 7-8 minutes of presented material. If you cannot present the portfolio in 7 minutes you have not internalized it yet.

Week two. The hostile-question gym. Yuri provides a library of 40 board questions known to be asked in real CAIO presentations. You record yourself answering 20 of them. You watch the recordings. You notice the three you flinch on. Those are the three you rehearse with a peer until you do not flinch.

Week three. The pre-wire simulation. You run a 30-minute conversation with each of your three peer panelists, in character. You are pre-wiring the simulation the way you would pre-wire a real board meeting. The point is to surface the objections you will face in the room so that the room becomes a confirmation, not a debate. Most candidates discover in this week that one of their core assumptions does not hold. Better to discover it here.

Week four. The dress rehearsal. A full 60-minute run with a different set of peer reviewers — not the ones who will be in the real room. Recorded, watched back, debriefed. You will hate watching yourself. Do it anyway. The two-hour debrief after the dress rehearsal is where the actual learning happens.

## Common failure modes

Five patterns account for almost every capstone failure.

Rambling. The candidate answers a 30-second question with a three-minute story. Directors stop listening at 45 seconds. The fix: every answer has a one-sentence headline, followed by at most two sentences of support. If they want more, they will ask.

Overpromising. The candidate, sensing a soft room, inflates the ROI case. The wildcard director catches it. The trust deficit cannot be repaired in the remaining 40 minutes. The fix: bracket every number with the assumption that would change it. Honest beats heroic in a board room.

Dodging the regulatory question. The candidate does not have a crisp answer on EU AI Act exposure, on sector-specific compliance (model risk for financial services, HIPAA-adjacent issues for healthcare AI), on the literacy mandate. The room concludes you are not ready. The fix: a 90-second answer on regulatory posture, rehearsed to the point of muscle memory, that you can deliver standing up at 3am.

No kill criteria. The candidate presents 12 initiatives and treats them all as winners. The first director who asks "which of these would you kill and when" exposes that there is no portfolio logic. The fix: every initiative in your portfolio has a kill condition written in advance — a metric, a date or a market signal that would cause you to wind it down. Show this in the deck.

The hedged ask. The candidate, sensing the budget is large, softens it. "$5M, but we could do meaningful work on $3M." The board concludes you do not believe in your own plan. The fix: present one number, defend it and accept a counter if it comes. Negotiation in the room is fine. Pre-negotiation in your own deck is not.

## What passing actually proves

Passing the capstone proves three things that no other credential on the market can prove.

It proves you can present a real AI strategy in a real board cadence and defend it under pressure from real directors. Most CAIO candidates have never done this before they walk into the real interview. You will have done it under harder conditions than the real interview.

It proves you have built the artifacts. The board memo, the operating model, the 100-day plan, the governance pack, the ROI model — these are the documents the next CEO will ask you to walk through in week one. You will arrive with versions already written. Hiring committees see the difference immediately.

It proves a third-party signal exists. The Yuri letter and the wildcard director letter together function as a performance reference from people who have seen you do the job in conditions designed to be harder than the real thing. In a market that is still inventing the credential, this is the credential.

The capstone is the moment the program stops being a course and becomes a license to operate. Pass it, and you walk into the next CAIO interview as the only candidate in the room who can prove they have already done the job.

<summary>Check yourself</summary>
**Q.** 18 minutes into your simulation. The wildcard director — a sitting public-company CAIO — interrupts: "I notice your portfolio has no kill criteria. How is this not the same plan that's failed at 95% of companies that tried it." Your real answer is that you have kill criteria but did not put them in the deck. What do you do?

**A.** You acknowledge the gap directly in one sentence: "You're right that the deck doesn't show them — that's a presentation choice I should reconsider." Then you give the kill criteria for two of the initiatives by memory, in 30 seconds total. Then you ask the director which one they would push hardest on, and walk through the kill protocol for that specific bet. You have turned a near-disqualifying gap into a demonstration of command. The pivot from dodging to engaging on the director's terms is the single highest-leverage move in any board room.
