## Why two documents, not one

A board doesn't read a deck the way an operator does. Directors skim the memo on the plane, form a position before the meeting and use the slides to confirm or refute that position. If your memo is weak, the slides cannot save you. If your slides are dense, the memo cannot save you either. You need both, and they have to say the same thing in different shapes.

The memo is the artifact of record. It is what gets forwarded to the audit committee, the CFO and the general counsel. It survives the meeting. The five-slide case is the live performance — it exists to anchor a 20-minute conversation, not to be read.

Treat them as one product with two surfaces. Write the memo first. The slides come out of it.

## What the 1-page board memo must answer

A board memo is one page. Not "one page if we shrink the font." One page in 11-point with margins a director can read on a phone. It answers five questions in this order.

**The so-what.** One sentence. Not "AI is transforming our industry." That is a TED talk, not a memo. Try: "Three of our four largest competitors have shipped a customer-facing AI product in the last 12 months and our renewal rate in the affected segment is down 4 points." The so-what names the threat or the opportunity in our specific business, in numbers a director already cares about.

**The cost of inaction.** Not the cost of acting — the cost of doing nothing. Boards are pattern-matching machines and the pattern they fear most is being the last incumbent to move. Quantify it: revenue at risk, cost line that grows, talent we will lose to competitors who pay an AI premium. Use a range, not a point estimate. "We estimate $18-32M of contribution margin at risk over 24 months if we hold the current operating model."

**The bet.** What we will do, in one paragraph. Name the portfolio, not the project. "We will stand up an AI office with a 12-initiative portfolio across three horizons, sequenced to ship two production wins in year one and reset our cost-to-serve by year three." A board needs to see that you have thought past the first demo.

**The governance answer.** This is the line that separates the operators who get hired from the ones who get politely thanked. "Our portfolio will be governed by a cross-functional AI risk committee chaired by the CAIO, with EU AI Act Article 4 literacy compliance, Article 6 high-risk classification reviews and a documented kill-switch protocol for every shipped model." If you cannot say this sentence in your sleep, you are not ready for the meeting.

**The 100-day arc.** What gets done by the next board meeting. Three to five concrete deliverables, each with a name attached. This is the line that signals you are an executive, not a consultant. Consultants present frameworks. Executives commit to milestones.

That's the page. Sign it. Date it. Distribute it 72 hours before the meeting.

## The 5-slide executive case

Five slides. Not six. The discipline is the point. Each slide answers exactly one of the questions in the memo and nothing else.

Slide one: the so-what, expressed as a single chart with one number on it. The number is the cost of inaction. If you cannot find one chart that carries the so-what, you don't understand the so-what yet.

Slide two: the bet. A 2x2 or a sequencing diagram showing the portfolio of initiatives across horizons. Not a project list. A portfolio. <span class="pl-stat">95%</span> of enterprise AI pilots produce zero ROI — the MIT NANDA figure — which means the board's default assumption is that this is a money pit. The portfolio view is what tells them you have a hit-rate strategy, not a one-shot bet.

Slide three: the governance and risk frame. The AI risk committee, the literacy training plan, the model inventory, the kill criteria. This is where the general counsel decides whether you are a peer or a problem. Use the regulatory regime they actually face — EU AI Act if you sell into Europe, sector-specific frameworks if you are in financial services or healthcare. Specificity reads as competence.

Slide four: the ask. One number for budget. One number for headcount. One sentence on reporting line. One sentence on decision rights. If your ask hedges, you lose. Boards respect a clean number they can debate. They distrust a range that signals you are unsure.

Slide five: the 100-day plan with named owners. The plan slide doubles as your accountability contract. When you come back next quarter, this is the slide you will be measured against.

## Framing mistakes that lose the room

I have watched dozens of these presentations. The losing patterns are consistent.

Techno-jargon. The moment you say "transformer architecture" or "RAG pipeline" to a board, you have lost the non-technical directors and bored the technical ones. Translate every technical term into a business outcome before it leaves your mouth.

The hedged ask. "We're thinking maybe $4-6M, depending on appetite" is a sentence that gets you $0. "We are asking for $5.2M against a $40M three-year revenue protection thesis" is a sentence that gets you $5.2M or a real negotiation.

No number. If you walk into a board meeting on AI strategy without a number — for ROI, for cost of inaction, for budget, for anything — you have signaled that you are not ready to be accountable. The number does not have to be precise. It has to exist.

The 40-slide deck. If you brought 40 slides, the board reads it as "this person cannot prioritize." The 5-slide discipline is a credibility signal in itself.

The unanswered governance question. The moment a director asks "what about hallucinations" or "what about the EU regulation," you have 15 seconds to demonstrate that you have already thought about it. Hesitation kills you.

## The pre-wire: reading the room before the meeting

The presentation is half the work. The other half is the conversation you have with each board member in the week before. This is called the pre-wire and it is non-negotiable.

Pull each director's calendar. Get 20 minutes with each one. Walk them through the memo. Ask one question: "What would you need to see to support this?" Then listen.

You are doing three things. First, you are surfacing objections before the meeting so you can address them in the deck instead of on the floor. Second, you are building a quorum of pre-committed supporters so the meeting becomes a confirmation, not a debate. Third, you are signaling that you are an operator who understands governance — directors hate being surprised in the room.

If you cannot pre-wire — because the board is hostile or you are an outside candidate presenting in a hiring round — get the agenda in advance, get the names of who will be in the room and prep for their known priors. Every director has a public record. Read it.

## Handling the hostile question

There will be one. It will come from the director who was skeptical from the start, or from the new director who needs to demonstrate independence. The question is usually a version of: "Why should we believe you can do this when everyone else is failing?"

The answer has three beats. First, acknowledge the failure rate — the 95% MIT figure is your friend here, because it tells the board you are not naive. Second, name the specific failure modes you will avoid: no pilots without a production path, no models in production without governance, no portfolio without kill criteria. Third, point to your evidence — a shipped artifact, a prior role, a portfolio of bets already in motion. The hostile question is your best slide. It is the moment the board decides whether to trust you.
