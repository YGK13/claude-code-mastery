## Why most AI ROI models collapse under questioning

Most AI business cases die for the same reason most strategy decks die. They confuse activity with value. The deck shows tokens processed, prompts written, models deployed, hours of training delivered. None of those are P&L line items. A CFO does not buy activity. She buys movement in a number she already tracks.

The other failure mode is the single-bucket pitch. Someone walks in with a $4M ask and a single benefit line called "productivity gains." That number is unfalsifiable, unattributable and will not survive contact with the audit committee. Sophisticated AI value models name five distinct buckets, assign each to a real owner and admit which ones are squishy.

The third failure is timeline laundering. The team books year-one savings against year-three implementation. The CFO catches it in twenty minutes. You lose credibility for the rest of the year.

A defensible model does the opposite. It names the buckets. It admits what is hard to attribute. It pre-empts the objections. And it ties every line to a P&L row a sitting controller can recognize.

## The five value buckets

Every AI investment, without exception, lives in some combination of five buckets. Your job is to name which ones you are claiming and weight them honestly.

**Revenue lift.** New revenue you would not have booked without the system. Better conversion in the funnel, faster sales cycle, new pricing power, a product feature you could not have shipped. The hardest to attribute and the most powerful when you can. Requires a control group or a clean pre/post cut.

**Cost takeout.** Headcount you do not have to backfill, vendor lines you can renegotiate or kill, infrastructure you decommission. This is where most pilots get pitched and where most lies get told. The honest version names the FTEs by role and the manager who has agreed in writing to not backfill.

**Risk reduction.** Fines you do not pay, lawsuits you do not lose, breaches that do not happen, regulatory penalties you avoid. Hard to quantify in advance, easy to quantify after the fact, and the bucket that makes the audit committee comfortable. Express as expected-loss-avoided using actuarial logic the CFO already uses for insurance.

**Speed and cycle time.** Time-to-close on a deal, time-to-resolve on a ticket, time-to-decision on a credit application. Only counts when the saved time converts into a P&L outcome. A salesperson who closes deals in 14 days instead of 21 only matters if they close more deals or you hire fewer salespeople.

**Optionality.** The bets you can now place because the capability exists. New markets, new products, new partnership architectures. Smallest near-term dollar value, largest strategic value, the bucket the board cares about most and the CFO trusts least. Express as a real option, not a deterministic forecast.

A clean model shows the dollar split across all five with a confidence rating on each. Anything claiming 80%+ in one bucket is either narrowly scoped or lying.

## Pre-mortems vs post-hocs

A pre-mortem is the document you write before the investment closes that names how this will fail. You list the three most likely reasons the value does not materialize, who would have to take what action to prevent each one and what early signal you will monitor. You then sign it and put it in the file.

This single artifact does more for your CFO credibility than any business case. It says, in writing, that you have thought about the downside. It commits you to the early indicators. And when the project drifts, you reach for the pre-mortem instead of arguing about whether the original numbers were right.

The post-hoc is the equivalent on the back end. Every quarter you publish the variance against the original model with the same five buckets. You name what came in low, what came in high and what you have changed in the next quarter's forecast. <span class="pl-stat">83%</span> of CEOs now say AI success depends on adoption not technology. The post-hoc is where you prove you are tracking the adoption side, not just the deployment.

## The value-tracking system

Models do not generate ROI. Behavior change does. So the tracking system has to connect three layers.

The first layer is model output. Tokens, calls, predictions, classifications. This is what every vendor dashboard shows you. It is necessary and almost entirely insufficient.

The second layer is workflow integration. Which humans took which action because of the model output. Did the sales rep send the email? Did the underwriter accept the credit recommendation? Did the engineer merge the suggested code? Without this layer you cannot connect the model to anything financial.

The third layer is the P&L line. Revenue per rep, loss ratio in the underwriting book, story points shipped per sprint, support cost per ticket. The lines already exist in the financial system. Your job is to instrument the second layer cleanly enough that the CFO trusts the attribution to the third.

When you build the system this way, the conversation with the CFO changes. You are no longer defending a forecast. You are showing her the live attribution chain and pointing at the variance.

## CFO objections you must pre-empt

Four objections will land in every meeting. Have the answer ready before she asks.

**Attribution.** "How do you know the AI caused that?" Answer with the control group, the pre/post window or the conservative haircut you applied. If you do not have one of those three, do not claim the value.

**Sustainment.** "Will the savings still be there in year three?" Answer with the operational ritual that maintains the change. A one-time deployment that disappears when a manager leaves is not real cost takeout.

**Lock-in.** "What happens when the vendor doubles the price?" Answer with the abstraction layer, the multi-model architecture and the renegotiation leverage. Pillar III covered the build pattern. This is where you show it.

**Cannibalization.** "Are we shifting revenue from one line to another?" Answer with the unit economics across both lines. If revenue moves from the consulting bucket to the software bucket, the gross margin should tell a clean story.

## Sizing a 3-year AI P&L without lying

A credible 3-year AI P&L has four characteristics. Year one is mostly investment with modest takeout, mostly in a single function where you have control. Year two is where the curve bends, with attributed value in three of the five buckets and a real adoption number behind it. Year three is the ambition case, with a sensitivity table and an explicit assumption about what would have to be true.

You publish the case with the assumptions visible. You attach the pre-mortem. You set the quarterly review cadence. You give the CFO a one-page variance report every 90 days. You become the executive in the C-suite she trusts on numbers.
